### [PROSECUTOR] (Round 1) · Citations: IPC 11, IPC 13 · Evidence: fact_13, fact_12, fact_2, fact_4, fact_1, fact_3, fact_14, fact_8, fact_9

### Legal Theory
May it please the Court. On behalf of the appellant-Corporation, I submit that the Labour Court’s order dated 19 September 2001 is wholly unsustainable in law.

### Submissions
1. That is a clear attempt to obtain regularisation through adjudication, which the Labour Court had no jurisdiction to grant merely by entertaining an application under those provisions. The Labour Court cannot create substantive service status contrary to the governing recruitment framework.
2. The appellant also consistently argued that the concerned workmen had not been selected through the process required for appointment of regular employees [fact_13]. This is decisive. Regularisation cannot be used to bypass the prescribed selection process and recruitment rules.
3. Further, the Corporation challenged the Labour Court’s order by writ petition, and even that writ petition was dismissed by the High Court [fact_12]. But dismissal of the writ petition does not cure the underlying jurisdictional defect if the Labour Court acted beyond the authority conferred by statute.
4. Here, the respondents were appointed on contract basis [fact_2]. The Labour Court nonetheless granted them regular and substantive status [fact_4]. That is not interpretation of standing orders; it is creation of a new service status, which is impermissible.
5. - the application before the Labour Court was not maintainable to the extent it sought regularisation [fact_1–fact_3];
- the workmen had not gone through the requisite selection process for regular appointment [fact_13];
- the Labour Court acted beyond jurisdiction in allowing the application [fact_14]; and
- the order dated 19 September 2001 deserves to be set aside [fact_4].
6. Accordingly, the appeal ought to be allowed.

### [DEFENSE] (Round 1) · Citations: IPC 11, IPC 13, IPC 10, IPC 4 · Evidence: fact_1, fact_2, fact_3, fact_15, fact_16, fact_17, fact_18, fact_19, fact_13, fact_20

### Legal Theory
The real dispute is jurisdictional, not criminal: the Labour Court’s order proceeded beyond the limited scope of Section 11-C of the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Disputes Act, and the prosecution-style claim of regularisation cannot stand merely because the workmen were on contract or had sought similar relief earlier.

### Submissions
1. The case itself shows that the application was made under Section 11-C of the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Disputes Act read with Section 13-A of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act [fact_1], and it sought a declaration of regular and substantive status with full regular-employee benefits [fact_2]-[fact_3]. The Supreme Court has already held that the scope of Section 11-C is much narrower than a reference under Section 10 of the Industrial Disputes Act or Section 4-K of the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Disputes Act [fact_15], and that the Labour Court could not grant the relief actually awarded [fact_16].
2. The record before the court does not contain any specific standing order of the Corporation that was actually identified or reproduced in the Labour Court order [fact_17]. Instead, the order referred to Rule 2 of the 1981 Regulations, which excluded contract employees [fact_18]. On that material, the Labour Court could not lawfully amend the regulations while deciding an application under Section 11-C [fact_19].
3. The appellant’s grievance that the concerned workmen had not been selected through the process required for regular appointment [fact_13] goes to the heart of the substantive claim. But that contention only reinforces that a regularisation-type declaration could not be granted in this proceeding. The Supreme Court ultimately allowed the appeals and set aside both the High Court judgment and the Labour Court order, leaving any grievance to be raised only through a proper reference under Section 4-K of the Uttar Pradesh Industrial Disputes Act or Section 10 of the Industrial Disputes Act if the State Government refers it [fact_20].

### Judicial Explanation
This record does not support anticipatory bail; it instead shows a concluded labour dispute in which the Supreme Court already set aside the Labour Court and High Court orders for want of jurisdiction. The Paragraph 24-style exception for abuse of process or lack of jurisdiction is not a basis to enlarge liberty here because the jurisdictional defect cuts against the labour award, not in favour of any applicant resisting lawful process. No individual-applicant differentiating facts are needed because the material concerns the legality of the adjudicatory forum’s order, not distinct overt acts by separate accused. There is also no Section 82/proclamation or absconding material to trigger the denial logic on that ground; the decisive point is simply that the challenged relief was beyond the Labour Court’s power.
