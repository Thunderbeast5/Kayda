### Legal Theory
The prosecution could not sustain the Labour Court’s wide relief on the basis of a limited standing-order proceeding; the record before the court shows only undisputed contract engagement, while the claim to regular status and regular pay required a proper industrial dispute adjudication. The key weakness is jurisdictional overreach and absence of the foundational certified standing order material needed to invoke the special standing-order remedy.

### Submissions
1. The Corporation’s core objection was that the concerned workmen were admittedly appointed on contract basis [fact_10], and the Labour Court nevertheless granted regular-status-related relief [fact_7, fact_8]. That approach is vulnerable because the relief sought was not merely interpretive; it altered service status and pay consequences, which the Supreme Court held could only be pursued in an appropriate industrial dispute proceeding, not by stretching the standing-order mechanism [fact_18, fact_19, fact_30, fact_31].
2. The record before the court does not contain any specific certified standing order text of the appellant, and the Court itself noted that the Labour Court’s order did not refer to any standing order of the appellant [standing_orders]. In the absence of the actual standing order provisions on record, the Labour Court could not conclusively assume a right to regularisation or minimum pay scales under Section 11-C of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act read with Section 13-A of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act [fact_4, fact_3].
3. On mens rea/ingredient analysis, the record does not show any deliberate statutory violation by the Corporation; at most, there was a legal dispute about the proper forum and the extent of the Labour Court’s powers [fact_11, fact_16, fact_18, fact_19]. Since the workmen were undisputedly on contract [fact_10], the essential foundation for treating them as regular employees was not established on the record before the Labour Court, and the Supreme Court therefore set aside the orders and left the parties to the proper industrial dispute remedy [fact_30, fact_31].

### [DEFENSE] (Round 2) · Citations: IPC 11, IPC 13, IPC 4, IPC 10 · Evidence: fact_4, fact_5, fact_6, fact_8, fact_30, fact_31, fact_3, fact_10, fact_11, fact_16, fact_19

### Legal Theory
The prosecution case is vulnerable on jurisdiction and proof: the Labour Court granted substantive relief without the statutory foundation of a certified standing order on record, and the dispute was in substance one requiring adjudication under the industrial dispute reference mechanism rather than under Section 11-C of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 / Section 13-A of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946.

### Submissions
1. The challenge to the Labour Court’s jurisdiction is substantial. Although the application was filed under Section 11-C of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 read with Section 13-A of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 [fact_4], the relief actually granted was a declaration of regular/substantive status and consequential pay benefits [fact_5][fact_6][fact_8]. That goes beyond mere interpretation or application of standing orders and instead invades the field reserved for a regular industrial dispute reference; the Supreme Court later accepted this submission and set aside both orders [fact_30][fact_31].
2. The record before the court does not contain any certified standing order text or any specific standing order of the appellant that could lawfully be interpreted or applied to confer regular status on the contract workers. The evidence inventory expressly marks standing orders as not_on_record, and the Court itself noted that the Labour Court’s order did not refer to any standing order of the appellant. In the absence of that foundational document, the Labour Court could not have validly exercised Section 11-C / Section 13-A jurisdiction [fact_3][fact_4].
3. On the merits, the Corporation’s position is that the 15 workmen were admittedly appointed on contract basis [fact_10][fact_11], and therefore they were not shown to have been selected through the process required for regular appointment [fact_16]. On these facts, the prosecution of the claim for regular pay scale and regular employee facilities does not establish the necessary legal foundation for substantive regularization; at most, it shows an admitted contractual engagement, which by itself does not satisfy the statutory conditions for regular status. The Supreme Court’s holding that the proper route was an industrial dispute reference under Section 4-K of the U.P. Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 or Section 10 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 underscores this jurisdictional and evidentiary gap [fact_19][fact_31].
