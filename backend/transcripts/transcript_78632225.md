### Legal Theory
May it please the Court, I strongly oppose this application for anticipatory bail.

### Submissions
1. This is not a case where the applicants came promptly before the court seeking protection in good faith. The record shows a deliberate and repeated failure to submit to the lawful process: summons were issued, bailable warrants followed, then non-bailable warrants, and finally a proclamation under Section 82 CrPC, with proceedings under Section 83 CrPC thereafter. Even after all of this, the applicants did not appear. They withdrew a bail-cum-surrender application fearing arrest and still chose not to surrender. That conduct speaks volumes.
2. The offences alleged are serious. The FIR invokes Sections 341, 323, 354, 354B, 379, 504, 506, and 149 IPC, along with Sections 3 and 4 of the Prevention of Witch (Daain) Practices Act, 1999. These are allegations of group assault, intimidation, humiliation, theft, and disrobing of women in a witch-hunting context. Such offences are not to be treated lightly, and anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC is an exceptional remedy, not one granted as a matter of course.
3. The Supreme Court in **Srikant Upadhyay v. State of Bihar, 2024 INSC 202** held that where an accused is absconding or concealing himself to avoid execution of a warrant and has been declared a proclaimed offender under Section 82 CrPC, he is ordinarily not entitled to anticipatory bail. The Court further held that a person lawfully summoned in a serious matter must submit to the authority of law and pursue lawful remedies rather than defy the process. That is exactly why this application must fail.
4. Here, custodial process is necessary not because of any abstract formula, but because the applicants have shown by their conduct that they will not appear voluntarily. Their repeated non-appearance after summons and warrants makes the coercive process under Sections 82 and 83 CrPC fully relevant. Granting anticipatory bail in such circumstances would undermine the authority of the court and reward evasion.
5. In view of the gravity of the offence, statutory presumptions, and the necessity of custodial interrogation, the prosecution humbly prays that this Anticipatory Bail application be rejected.
- **Section 438 CrPC**: anticipatory bail; an exceptional pre-arrest protection, not a matter of course.
- **Section 82 CrPC**: proclamation when an accused absconds or conceals himself to avoid execution of process.
- **Section 83 CrPC**: attachment proceedings following proclamation.
- **Section 167 CrPC**: not directly central on these facts, but relevant to custody/remand principles generally; however, the present record turns primarily on the applicants’ conduct under Sections 82/83 and their attempt to invoke Section 438 CrPC.
- **IPC 341**: wrongful restraint.
- **IPC 323**: voluntarily causing hurt.
- **IPC 354**: assault or criminal force to woman with intent to outrage modesty.
- **IPC 354B**: assault or use of criminal force to woman with intent to disrobe.
- **IPC 379**: theft.
- **IPC 504**: intentional insult with intent to provoke breach of the peace.
- **IPC 506**: criminal intimidation.
- **IPC 149**: every member of unlawful assembly guilty of offence committed in prosecution of common object.
- **Prevention of Witch (Daain) Practices Act, 1999, Sections 3 and 4**: invoked in the FIR for the alleged witch-hunting related attack and humiliation.
Here, the applicants were named in FIR No. 79 of 2020 for serious offences under IPC Sections 341, 323, 354, 354B, 379, 504, 506, and 149, along with Sections 3 and 4 of the Prevention of Witch (Daain) Practices Act, 1999. The allegations are not trivial: the complainant-side version speaks of a group attack, abuse, physical assault, disrobing of women, and humiliation by forcing dung into the mouth of an elderly woman. Such allegations directly engage the protective object of the penal provisions invoked.
6. Most importantly, the applicants’ own conduct disentitles them to anticipatory bail. They did not appear after summons; they remained absent even after bailable warrants; they withdrew a bail-cum-surrender application fearing arrest; they then failed to appear even after non-bailable warrants were issued; and after proclamation under Section 82 CrPC, they still did not submit to process. Proceedings under Section 83 CrPC were then initiated. This is precisely the kind of conduct that **[CASE: Srikant Upadhyay v. State of Bihar, 2024 INSC 202]** says ordinarily bars anticipatory bail.
7. The argument that their application was pending before the High Court when Section 82 proceedings were issued does not help them. The factual record shows a pattern of avoiding the trial court’s lawful directions, not bona fide pursuit of remedy. Once they had actual knowledge of coercive process, they still did not appear or challenge the proclamation in time. On these facts, Section 438 CrPC cannot be used as a shield against consequences of abscondence-like conduct.

### Legal Theory
The prosecution case, even on its own showing, is materially weakened on proof, attribution, and mens rea; the record also shows that the more serious allegations were not carried through to the charge sheet, and the pre-arrest bail issue turns on legal consequence of process under CrPC 82/83 rather than automatic guilt.

### Submissions
1. The complainant-version alleges a group assault and humiliation, but the investigation did not sustain the full FIR narrative: the charge sheet was filed only under IPC 341, IPC 323, and IPC 504, and only against accused Lakhpati Kunwar [fact_39]. That narrowed police conclusion creates a real doubt about whether the broader allegations under IPC 354, IPC 354B, IPC 379, IPC 506, IPC 149 and the Daain Act were sufficiently supported on record. At the bail stage, such a divergence between FIR allegations and the final investigative outcome goes directly to the weight of the prosecution case.
2. The record before the court does not contain any medical or MLC report [fact_15]. In a case alleging physical assault, forced feeding of dung, vomiting, falling down, tearing of clothes, and disrobing [fact_34] [fact_36] [fact_38], the absence of medical corroboration in the record weakens proof of actual injury, the nature of force used, and whether the incident rose to the level alleged. For the specific non-bailable and aggravated allegations, the prosecution has failed to produce on record independent corroboration of injury or forensic support, so the court cannot assume the highest version of events as established.
3. Mens rea is also not established with the precision required by the invoked offences. Under IPC 504 and IPC 506, the prosecution must show intentional insult or criminal intimidation with the requisite intent to provoke breach of peace or cause alarm; however, the facts as recorded are largely a quarrel narrative surrounding a prior family/property dispute, including earlier FIRs and a civil suit [fact_26] [fact_27] [fact_28], which suggests possible background enmity and exaggeration rather than clear proof of specific intent. Likewise, the allegation under IPC 354B depends on proof of assault or use of criminal force with intent to disrobe; the present material contains accusations [fact_36] [fact_38], but the prosecution has not, on the record excerpt, supplied the kind of independent evidence that would eliminate reasonable doubt on attribution, common object under IPC 149, and the precise mental element of each appellant.
4. As to the procedural posture under CrPC 438, the appellants rely on the fact that their anticipatory bail application had been filed before the later proclamation steps [fact_49] [fact_90] [fact_91]. The prosecution relies on CrPC 82 and CrPC 83, but those steps were taken after repeated non-appearance [fact_58] [fact_59]. That may justify adverse inference on conduct, yet it still does not cure the prosecution’s underlying evidentiary gaps on the substantive offences; under the binding ratio in Srikant Upadhyay v. State of Bihar, 2024 INSC 202, anticipatory bail is exceptional and ordinarily not available where a person is absconding after proclamation under CrPC 82 [srikant_para2-3], but the court must still be cautious not to convert process-default into conclusive proof of guilt.

### Judicial Explanation
This application is denied because the controlling holding in Srikant Upadhyay applies: the appellants had summons, bailable warrants, non-bailable warrants, and then a proclamation under Section 82, yet still did not submit to process. The Paragraph 24 exception for extreme, exceptional cases does not assist them, since there is no challenge showing the warrant/proclamation was without jurisdiction or a malicious abuse of process; rather, the record reflects a lawful progression of coercive steps after repeated non-appearance. The individual allegations also differ as to each applicant, with specific overt acts alleged against Appellant No. 2, Appellant No. 4, and Appellant No. 5, so this is not a case of bare omnibus accusation. The pendency of the anticipatory bail application, without interim protection, does not cure defiance of process or prevent Section 82/83 steps, and the appeal therefore fails.
